# Module 1 — Scale and Structure of the Universe

## From Earth to the Observable Universe

---

## 1. Why Cosmic Scale Is Difficult to Imagine

We live on a planet whose size already feels enormous.

Earth has an equatorial diameter of about **12,742 km**. Crossing a large country can take many hours, and travelling around Earth requires tens of thousands of kilometres.

Yet Earth is only one small part of a much larger hierarchy of structures.

Earth belongs to the **Solar System**.
The Solar System is part of the **Milky Way Galaxy**.
The Milky Way belongs to the **Local Group of galaxies**.
The Local Group is part of a much larger structure associated with the **Laniakea Supercluster**.
And beyond these structures lies the enormous **observable universe**.

The difficulty is that our familiar units - metres and kilometres, quickly become inconvenient.

For example:

* Earth–Moon distance: about **384,400 km**
* Earth–Sun distance: about **149.6 million km**
* distance to the nearest star beyond the Sun: more than **40 trillion km**
* diameter of the Milky Way: about **100,000 light-years**
* radius of the observable universe: about **46.5 billion light-years**

The numbers become so large that simply writing them in kilometres stops being useful.

This is why astronomy uses a hierarchy of distance units.

---

# 2. The Units Astronomers Use

## 2.1 Kilometres

A kilometre is useful for distances within the Earth–Moon system and for many terrestrial measurements.

For example:

$$
1\ \mathrm{km}=10^3\ \mathrm{m}
$$

The average distance from Earth to the Moon is approximately:

$$
384,400\ \mathrm{km}
$$

That number is large, but still manageable.

Once we start discussing distances between planets and stars, however, kilometres become cumbersome.

---

# 3. The Astronomical Unit — AU

The **astronomical unit (AU)** is primarily useful for describing distances within the Solar System.

One astronomical unit is defined from the Earth–Sun distance:

$$
1\ \mathrm{AU}=149,597,870.7\ \mathrm{km}
$$

or approximately:

$$
1\ \mathrm{AU}\approx1.496\times10^8\ \mathrm{km}
$$

Instead of saying that a planet is hundreds of millions or billions of kilometres away, astronomers can describe its distance in AU.

For example, Earth is approximately:

$$
1\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

from the Sun.

The advantage is immediately obvious.

Rather than writing a huge number in kilometres, we can say:

> Earth is approximately 1 AU from the Sun.

---

## 3.1 Why AU Is Useful

The AU gives us a convenient scale for the Solar System.

The planets do not sit at equal distances from the Sun. Their orbital distances increase dramatically as we move outward.

The outermost of the eight planets, Neptune, reaches approximately:

$$
30.1\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

from the Sun.

This means Neptune's distance from the Sun is roughly thirty times the Earth–Sun distance.

The important idea is that **AU is a Solar-System-scale unit**.

When we move beyond the Solar System, even AU eventually becomes inconvenient.

That is where the light-year becomes useful.

---

# 4. The Light-Year

The name can be misleading.

A **light-year is a unit of distance, not time**.

It is the distance that light travels in one Julian year.

Approximately:

$$
1\ \mathrm{light\text{-}year}
\approx9.4607\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{km}
$$

That is about **9.46 trillion kilometres**.

So when astronomers say:

> A star is 4 light-years away,

they mean that the distance to that star is approximately four light-years.

They are not saying that the star is "four years old."

---

## 4.1 Why Light-Years Are Useful

The light-year becomes useful when distances become much larger than the scale of the Solar System.

For example, the nearest stellar system to the Solar System is the **Alpha Centauri system**, at approximately:

$$
4.37\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

The star **Proxima Centauri** is approximately:

$$
4.24\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

away.

Using kilometres would require enormous numbers.

Using light-years makes the scale much easier to communicate.

---

# 5. The Parsec

Another important astronomical unit is the **parsec**, abbreviated **pc**.

A parsec is defined geometrically:

> It is the distance at which an astronomical unit subtends an angle of one arcsecond.

Its approximate length is:

$$
1\ \mathrm{pc}\approx3.0857\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{km}
$$

A parsec is also related to the light-year:

$$
1\ \mathrm{pc}\approx3.26\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

Parsecs and their larger versions—kiloparsecs (kpc) and megaparsecs (Mpc)—are particularly useful when describing galactic and cosmological distances.

For example, the Sun is approximately:

$$
8\ \mathrm{kpc}
$$

from the centre of the Milky Way.

---

# 6. Understanding the Scale Through a Model

One way to appreciate the enormous differences between astronomical scales is to imagine shrinking everything dramatically.

Suppose Earth were represented by a small object approximately **1.9 cm in diameter**.

On such a scale, the Sun would be represented by an object roughly **2 m in diameter**.

But the important part is not just the size of the objects.

The distance between them would also have to be scaled.

The Sun would be more than **200 m away** from the model Earth.

Now consider the nearest star.

On this same scale, Proxima Centauri would be tens of thousands of kilometres away—more than **55,000 km** according to the model described in the research material.

This illustrates an important feature of cosmic structure:

> **Objects become separated by enormous distances compared with their own sizes.**

The universe is not simply a collection of large objects packed together.

Much of the universe is extremely empty.

---

# 7. Earth and the Moon

We can begin our cosmic hierarchy at home.

Earth has an equatorial diameter of approximately:

$$
12,742\ \mathrm{km}
$$

The Moon has a diameter of approximately:

$$
3,475\ \mathrm{km}
$$

The average distance between Earth and the Moon is:

$$
384,400\ \mathrm{km}
$$

This distance corresponds to approximately:

$$
1.28\ \mathrm{light\text{-}seconds}
$$

In other words, light takes a little over one second to travel from Earth to the Moon.

Another useful comparison is that the average Earth–Moon distance is roughly **30 Earth diameters**.

So even our nearest major astronomical neighbour is already separated from us by a distance many times Earth's own diameter.

---

# 8. The Solar System

Earth and the Moon are part of the Solar System.

The Solar System contains the Sun and the objects gravitationally associated with it, including the eight planets.

The planetary system extends much farther than Earth's orbit.

Neptune, the outermost of the eight planets, reaches approximately:

$$
30.1\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

from the Sun.

However, this does **not** mean that the Solar System suddenly ends at Neptune.

The Solar System contains much more distant material.

---

# 9. The Heliosphere

The Sun continuously releases charged particles into space in what is known as the **solar wind**.

The solar wind creates a large region around the Sun called the **heliosphere**.

According to the research material, the heliosphere extends to approximately:

$$
100\text{–}120\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

in radius.

This is substantially farther than Neptune's orbit.

Therefore, when thinking about the scale of the Solar System, we should not imagine the eight planets as defining its complete physical extent.

The Solar System contains regions extending far beyond the planetary orbits.

---

# 10. The Oort Cloud

Even beyond the heliosphere lies another important region associated with the Solar System: the **Oort Cloud**.

The Oort Cloud is thought to be an extremely distant reservoir of icy bodies.

The research material describes an inner region called the **Hills Cloud**, approximately:

$$
2,000\text{–}5,000\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

from the Sun.

The outer Oort Cloud may extend to approximately:

$$
50,000\text{–}100,000\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

Some estimates place its outer extent at around:

$$
1.6\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

or approximately:

$$
0.5\ \mathrm{pc}
$$

The Oort Cloud is particularly important because it is considered a source region for **long-period comets**.

It also illustrates how misleading it can be to think of the Solar System as simply:

> Sun + eight planets.

The gravitational domain associated with the Sun extends vastly farther.

---

# 11. The Local Stellar Neighborhood

After moving beyond the Solar System, we enter the much larger neighbourhood of nearby stars.

A region extending roughly **5–10 light-years** around the Sun contains a few dozen stars.

Some important nearby stellar systems include:

### Proxima Centauri

Proxima Centauri is approximately:

$$
4.24\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

away.

### Alpha Centauri

The Alpha Centauri system is approximately:

$$
4.37\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

away.

### Barnard's Star

Barnard's Star is approximately:

$$
6\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

away.

The enormous distances between these stars are important.

Even though stars may appear close together when viewed from Earth, the actual distances between them are immense.

---

# 12. The Milky Way Galaxy

Our Solar System is itself only a tiny part of a much larger structure: the **Milky Way Galaxy**.

The Milky Way is a **barred spiral galaxy**.

It contains an estimated:

$$
100\text{–}400\ \mathrm{billion}
$$

stars.

Its diameter is approximately:

$$
100,000\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

The stellar disk has a thickness on the order of:

$$
1\text{–}3\ \mathrm{kpc}
$$

This gives us an enormous jump in scale.

The distance between nearby stars is measured in light-years.

The size of the entire galaxy is measured in tens of thousands of light-years.

---

# 13. Where Is the Solar System Inside the Milky Way?

The Solar System is not located at the centre of the Milky Way.

The Sun is approximately:

$$
8\ \mathrm{kpc}
$$

from the Galactic Centre.

This corresponds to roughly:

$$
26,000\ \mathrm{light\text{-}years}
$$

The Solar System lies within a region of the Milky Way associated with the **Orion Arm**.

So our location can be described hierarchically:

**Earth**

→ **Solar System**

→ **Orion Arm**

→ **Milky Way Galaxy**

---

# 14. The Sun's Motion Through the Galaxy

The Sun is not stationary within the Milky Way.

It travels around the Galactic Centre at approximately:

$$
220\ \mathrm{km/s}
$$

A complete orbit around the Galactic Centre takes approximately:

$$
220\ \mathrm{million\ years}
$$

This gives us another useful perspective.

Earth completes an orbit around the Sun in approximately one year.

The Sun, together with the Solar System, takes hundreds of millions of years to complete one orbit around the centre of the Milky Way.

---

# 15. The Local Group

The Milky Way is not an isolated galaxy.

It belongs to a larger collection of galaxies known as the **Local Group**.

The Local Group contains more than **50 galaxies**.

Among its most prominent members are:

* the Milky Way
* the Andromeda Galaxy
* numerous smaller galaxies

The Milky Way and Andromeda are approximately:

$$
2.5\ \mathrm{million\ light\text{-}years}
$$

apart.

The Local Group spans roughly:

$$
10\ \mathrm{million\ light\text{-}years}
$$

in diameter.

---

## 15.1 Why Doesn't the Local Group Expand Like the Universe?

The universe as a whole is expanding.

However, gravitationally bound systems do not simply expand along with the overall cosmic expansion.

Within the Local Group, gravity is strong enough to bind the galaxies together.

Therefore, the Local Group provides an example of a smaller-scale structure that is gravitationally bound even though the universe on much larger scales is expanding.

---

# 16. Superclusters

The Local Group is itself part of a much larger cosmic environment.

For a long time, astronomers used the term **Virgo Supercluster** for a large region of galaxies associated with the Virgo Cluster.

The research material describes the Virgo Supercluster as approximately:

$$
100\text{–}150\ \mathrm{million\ light\text{-}years}
$$

across.

However, large-scale mapping of galaxy motions has led to a broader structure being discussed: **Laniakea**.

---

# 17. Laniakea

Laniakea is a vast region of galaxies defined in part through the large-scale motions of galaxies.

It spans approximately:

$$
520\ \mathrm{million\ light\text{-}years}
$$

and contains roughly:

$$
100,000\ \mathrm{galaxies}
$$

The Milky Way lies near the outskirts of this enormous structure.

The galaxies in this region show large-scale flows toward a region associated with the **Great Attractor**.

The important idea is that cosmic structures are nested.

We are not simply:

> Earth → universe

Instead, we can build a hierarchy:

**Earth**

→ **Solar System**

→ **Local Stellar Neighborhood**

→ **Milky Way**

→ **Local Group**

→ **Larger galaxy structures**

→ **Laniakea**

→ **Observable Universe**

---

# 18. The Observable Universe

At the largest scale considered here, we reach the **observable universe**.

The observable universe is the region from which light or other information has had enough time to reach us since the beginning of cosmic history.

Its present-day radius is approximately:

$$
46.5\ \mathrm{billion\ light\text{-}years}
$$

giving a diameter of approximately:

$$
93\ \mathrm{billion\ light\text{-}years}
$$

This may initially seem contradictory.

If the universe is approximately **13.8 billion years old**, how can the observable universe have a radius of approximately **46.5 billion light-years**?

The answer lies in the expansion of the universe.

Light has been travelling toward us for billions of years, while the space through which it travels has itself been expanding.

Therefore, the present-day distance to the location from which that light originated can be much greater than simply:

> 13.8 billion light-years.

The observable-universe distance is therefore not just calculated by multiplying the age of the universe by the speed of light.

---

# 19. Does the Observable Universe Have a Centre?

It is important not to interpret the observable universe as a giant sphere with Earth physically sitting at its special central location.

The observable universe is centred on **the observer's location** because it represents the region from which signals can reach that observer.

Another observer in another sufficiently distant location would have their own observable universe.

This is connected with the large-scale concepts of **homogeneity** and **isotropy**.

### Homogeneity

On sufficiently large scales, the universe is treated as statistically similar from place to place.

### Isotropy

On sufficiently large scales, the universe appears statistically similar in different directions.

These concepts do not mean that every location in the universe is identical.

They describe the large-scale statistical structure of the universe.

---

# 20. There Is No Special Cosmic Centre

The hierarchical picture can sometimes create a misleading mental image.

It may seem as though:

> Earth is inside the Solar System,
> the Solar System is inside the Milky Way,
> the Milky Way is inside Laniakea,
> and therefore Laniakea must be sitting at some particular centre of the universe.

That is not the correct interpretation.

The observable universe is not being presented as a structure with Earth at a physically privileged cosmic centre.

Rather, Earth is simply the location from which **our observable universe** is defined.

---

# 21. Cosmic Scale as Powers of Ten

One of the easiest ways to appreciate the enormous range of scales in astronomy is through powers of ten.

Some representative scales are:

| Object / Distance          |              Approximate Scale |
| -------------------------- | -----------------------------: |
| Human                      |    $1.7\times10^0\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| Earth                      |    $1.3\times10^7\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| 1 AU                       | $1.5\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| Solar System to Neptune    | $4.5\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| 1 light-year               | $9.5\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| Milky Way                  | $1.0\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| Observable universe radius | $4.4\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{m}$ |

These numbers show that astronomical scale is not simply "a little larger" than human scale.

The difference spans many orders of magnitude.

---

# 22. From Human Scale to the Observable Universe

A human is roughly:

$$
1.7\ \mathrm{m}
$$

tall.

The observable universe has a radius of approximately:

$$
4.4\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{m}
$$

The difference between these scales is approximately **27 orders of magnitude**.

That means the observable universe's radius is enormously larger than the scale of an individual human.

Even moving from Earth to a distance of one light-year represents approximately **nine orders of magnitude** in scale.

This is why astronomy relies heavily on scientific notation and specialized distance units.

---

# 23. A Complete Cosmic Address

We can now construct a kind of cosmic address for Earth.

Starting locally:

**Earth**

↓

**Earth–Moon system**

↓

**Solar System**

↓

**Local Stellar Neighborhood**

↓

**Milky Way Galaxy**

↓

**Local Group**

↓

**Larger supercluster-scale structure**

↓

**Laniakea**

↓

**Observable Universe**

Each step represents a dramatic increase in spatial scale.

The key point is not merely memorizing the names.

It is understanding that the universe has a **nested structure**, with smaller systems existing within progressively larger environments.

---

# 24. The Universe Is Mostly Empty

Another important consequence of these scales is that space is extraordinarily dilute.

The distances between astronomical objects are enormous compared with the sizes of the objects themselves.

For example, stars within a galaxy can be separated by several light-years, while the stars themselves are vastly smaller than those distances.

Likewise, planets occupy only tiny regions compared with the distances between planetary orbits.

The Solar System is therefore not a densely packed collection of planets.

It is mostly empty space.

This idea becomes increasingly important as we move outward through the cosmic hierarchy.

# 26. The Big Picture

The most important lesson of this module is not a single number.

It is the **change in scale**.

We begin with something familiar:

> **Earth**

Then we progressively zoom outward:

> Earth
> → Solar System
> → nearby stars
> → Milky Way
> → Local Group
> → larger galaxy structures
> → Laniakea
> → observable universe

At every step, the relevant distance unit changes because the previous unit becomes inconvenient.

We move from:

**kilometres**

to

**astronomical units**

to

**light-years**

to

**parsecs and kiloparsecs**

and eventually to the enormous scales used in cosmology.

The result is a picture in which Earth is not an isolated object, but one component of a deeply nested cosmic hierarchy.

## Core Takeaway

The universe is organized across an enormous hierarchy of scales.

**Earth is part of the Solar System.
The Solar System is part of the Milky Way.
The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group.
The Local Group exists within still larger cosmic structures.
All of these structures are part of the observable universe.**

Understanding astronomy therefore requires learning not only what these objects are, but also **how their scales relate to one another**.
