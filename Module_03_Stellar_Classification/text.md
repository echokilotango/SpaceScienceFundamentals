# Module 3 — Stellar Classification and the Hertzsprung–Russell Diagram

## 1. The Cosmic Map: Introduction to the Hertzsprung–Russell Diagram

Imagine that we want to write the biography of a star.

A star is not a static object. It is born, changes its internal structure, spends different periods of its life in different physical states, and eventually reaches a final evolutionary state. But how can we tell where a star is in this long story?

One of the most powerful tools for doing this is the **Hertzsprung–Russell diagram**, usually called the **H-R diagram**.

The H-R diagram can be thought of as a **cosmic map**. Instead of plotting the physical location of a star in space, it plots properties of the star itself. By locating a star on this map, we can infer information about its internal structural state and its evolutionary progress.

The two primary quantities used to define this map are:

* **Luminosity** — the total energy output per unit time.
* **Effective temperature** — the temperature characterizing the radiation from the stellar surface.

The important idea is that these are **external properties** that tell us something about what is happening **inside** the star.

Energy generated or transported within the stellar interior eventually reaches the surface, or photosphere, where it is radiated away. The energy transport inside the star is driven by temperature gradients. Therefore, what we observe at the surface is connected to the physical processes occurring throughout the stellar interior.

The H-R diagram takes those observable properties and uses them to locate a star within its evolutionary biography — from its early contraction through later stages and eventually to its final remains.

---

## 2. What Exactly Are the Two Axes?

Before using the H-R diagram, we need to understand what its two fundamental quantities actually mean.

### 2.1 Luminosity

Luminosity is the amount of energy emitted by a star per unit time.

More precisely, the research document describes luminosity as the **net energy per second passing outward through a sphere of radius $r$**.

In technical stellar-structure literature, this quantity may be written as $L_r$ or $M_r$ in the referenced notation. At the stellar surface, it becomes the total stellar luminosity, written as $L$.

So when we talk about a star being more luminous, we are talking about its intrinsic energy output.

---

### 2.2 Effective Temperature

The second major quantity is the **effective temperature**.

This is the temperature associated with the radiation emitted by the stellar surface.

It is especially useful because the temperature of a star affects the wavelengths of radiation it emits.

Hotter stars emit radiation at shorter wavelengths and therefore tend toward the blue end of the visible spectrum.

Cooler stars emit radiation at longer wavelengths and tend toward the red end.

So the effective temperature gives us a bridge between:

**the physical condition of the star → the radiation it emits → the way its spectrum appears.**

This is why temperature becomes the foundation for stellar spectral classification.

---

# 3. The Spectral Alphabet: O, B, A, F, G, K, M

One of the first things students encounter in stellar astronomy is the sequence:

**O – B – A – F – G – K – M**

It can initially look like an arbitrary alphabetical list.

It is not.

The sequence is fundamentally a **temperature sequence**, running from the hottest stars to the coolest stars.

But temperature does more than determine colour.

The physical state of matter in a stellar atmosphere changes with temperature. In particular, the degree to which atoms are ionized changes.

The research document connects this behaviour to the **Boltzmann and Saha formulae**. At high temperatures, atoms can become stripped of electrons, producing highly ionized states. At lower temperatures, bound states become more favourable.

Eventually, at sufficiently low temperatures, molecules can form.

This is particularly important for M-type stars, where molecules such as **titanium oxide (TiO)** can form and produce distinctive absorption bands in their spectra.

Thus, the spectral classes are not merely labels.

They correspond to different physical conditions in stellar atmospheres.

---

# 4. O-Type Stars — The Hottest End

O-type stars occupy the hottest end of the sequence.

Their effective temperatures are:

**greater than 30,000 K**

At these extremely high temperatures, ionization is strong. One of their defining spectral characteristics is the presence of strong lines from ionized helium, particularly **He II**.

They are associated with a **blue colour**.

So the O class can be summarized as:

* Temperature: **greater than 30,000 K**
* Very high ionization
* Strong ionized helium lines, He II
* Blue colour

The important conceptual point is that the spectral signature is a consequence of the physical state of matter at these temperatures.

---

# 5. B-Type Stars

B-type stars are cooler than O-type stars but remain very hot.

Their temperature range is approximately:

**10,000–30,000 K**

Their spectra are characterized by **neutral helium lines**, designated He I.

They tend toward a **blue-white colour**.

Therefore:

* O → extraordinarily hot
* B → very hot

and the temperature decreases as we move through the sequence.

---

# 6. A-Type Stars

A-type stars have temperatures around:

**7,500–10,000 K**

One of their most important defining characteristics is the presence of the **strongest hydrogen lines of the Balmer series**.

Their colour is described as **white**.

This gives us an important example of why stellar classification works: changing temperature changes the physical state of atoms in the stellar atmosphere, and therefore changes which spectral lines become prominent.

---

# 7. F-Type Stars

F-type stars are cooler than A-type stars.

Their temperature range is approximately:

**6,000–7,500 K**

Their defining spectral characteristics include:

* Ionized calcium, Ca II
* Neutral metals

They are associated with a **yellow-white colour**.

---

# 8. G-Type Stars

G-type stars have temperatures around:

**5,200–6,000 K**

This is the spectral class containing the Sun, which is why the research document describes this class as **Sun-like**.

Their spectra contain strong Ca II lines as well as neutral metals.

They are associated with a **yellow colour**.

The important thing to remember is that the Sun's classification as a G-type star is not based simply on its colour. Its spectral characteristics and effective temperature place it within this classification.

---

# 9. K-Type Stars

K-type stars are cooler than G-type stars.

Their temperature range is approximately:

**3,700–5,200 K**

Their spectra are dominated by **neutral metal lines**.

They tend toward an **orange colour**.

---

# 10. M-Type Stars — The Coolest End

M-type stars occupy the coolest end of the sequence.

Their temperatures are:

**less than 3,700 K**

At these temperatures, the physical conditions allow molecules to form in the stellar atmosphere.

A particularly important example is **titanium oxide, TiO**.

These molecules produce distinctive molecular absorption bands.

M-type stars therefore have a **red colour** and characteristic molecular spectral features.

This provides a particularly clear demonstration that the O-B-A-F-G-K-M sequence is really a sequence of changing physical conditions.

---

# 11. The Complete Spectral Sequence

We can now see the complete sequence:

**O → B → A → F → G → K → M**

from **hot to cool**.

The important temperature ranges are:

| Spectral Class | Approximate Temperature | Defining Spectral Characteristic | Colour       |
| -------------- | ----------------------: | -------------------------------- | ------------ |
| O              |              > 30,000 K | Strong ionized helium, He II     | Blue         |
| B              |         10,000–30,000 K | Neutral helium, He I             | Blue-white   |
| A              |          7,500–10,000 K | Strongest Balmer hydrogen lines  | White        |
| F              |           6,000–7,500 K | Ca II and neutral metals         | Yellow-white |
| G              |           5,200–6,000 K | Strong Ca II and neutral metals  | Yellow       |
| K              |           3,700–5,200 K | Dominant neutral metal lines     | Orange       |
| M              |               < 3,700 K | Molecular bands such as TiO      | Red          |

These characteristics come directly from the research document's spectral-classification table.

The sequence should therefore be understood as a **physical progression**, not something to memorize without meaning.

---

# 12. Why Does Temperature Change the Spectrum?

The connection can now be built step by step.

A star has a particular surface temperature.

That temperature determines the physical state of the atoms and molecules in its atmosphere.

At very high temperatures, atoms become highly ionized.

At intermediate temperatures, particular atomic states and transitions become prominent.

At sufficiently low temperatures, molecules can form.

These different states produce different absorption features in the spectrum.

Therefore, when astronomers examine a stellar spectrum, the spectral lines provide information about the physical temperature and state of the stellar atmosphere.

This is why the sequence

**O–B–A–F–G–K–M**

is fundamentally a temperature sequence.

---

# 13. The Starting Line: The Zero-Age Main Sequence

The H-R diagram is not merely a classification diagram.

It can also be used to follow stellar evolution.

One of the most important reference locations is the **Zero-Age Main Sequence**, or **ZAMS**.

The research document describes the ZAMS as the **“adult starting line”** of a star's biography.

Why is it called the starting line?

A newly forming star undergoes an initial contraction phase.

During this period, gravitational contraction is important to its evolution. Eventually, the initial contraction ends and the **nuclear timescale**, $\tau_n$, takes over from the **Kelvin–Helmholtz timescale**, $\tau_{KH}$.

That transition marks the star's arrival on the ZAMS.

So the ZAMS is not simply a line drawn arbitrarily on the H-R diagram.

It represents a physical stage in the life of a star.

---

# 14. Hydrostatic Equilibrium on the ZAMS

Once the star reaches the ZAMS, it maintains stability through **hydrostatic equilibrium**.

The basic physical picture is a balance:

**gravity pulls inward**

while

**internal pressure supports the star against that inward pull.**

The research document describes this as internal pressure offsetting the inward pull of gravity.

This balance can be expressed mathematically.

In Eulerian form:

$\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} = -g\rho = -\frac{Gm}{r^2}\rho$

Here:

* $P$ is pressure.
* $r$ is distance from the centre.
* $g$ is gravitational acceleration.
* $\rho$ is density.
* $G$ is the gravitational constant.
* $m$ is the mass contained within radius $r$.

The same condition can also be expressed using the mass coordinate $m$, giving the Lagrangian form:

$\frac{\partial P}{\partial m} = -\frac{Gm}{4\pi r^4}$

These are two ways of describing the same fundamental condition of stellar mechanical equilibrium.

---

# 15. What Forces Are in Balance?

The hydrostatic-equilibrium picture becomes more complete when we consider the different contributions to the pressure.

The inward gravitational force is determined by the mass distribution within the star.

Against this inward tendency, the star has internal pressure.

The research document identifies the gas pressure as consisting of:

$P_{gas} = P_{ion} + P_e$

where $P_{ion}$ represents ion pressure and $P_e$ represents electron pressure.

There is also a contribution from radiation pressure:

$P_{rad} = \frac{1}{3}aT^4$

where $a$ is the radiation density constant and $T$ is temperature.

Thus, when we say that a star is supported by “pressure,” that pressure can have different physical contributions.

---

# 16. Radiation Pressure

Radiation pressure is given by:

$P_{rad} = \frac{1}{3}aT^4$

The radiation density constant used in the research document is:

$a = 7.57 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{erg\ cm^{-3}\ K^{-4}}$

The important feature of the equation is the $T^4$ dependence.

This means radiation pressure is strongly connected to temperature.

As we study more massive and hotter stars, radiation pressure therefore becomes an important part of the stellar-structure picture.

---

# 17. Mass Is Destiny: The Mass–Luminosity Connection

Now we arrive at one of the central ideas of the research document:

> **A star's initial mass acts as its chronometer.**

In other words, mass helps determine the pace at which the star's evolutionary biography unfolds.

Why?

The mass of the star determines the gravitational conditions inside it.

A more massive star requires greater pressure to prevent gravitational collapse.

That higher pressure is associated with a higher central temperature.

The result is that the internal conditions of the star are strongly influenced by its initial mass.

---

# 18. Central Pressure

The research document gives an approximate expression for the central pressure:

$P_c \approx \frac{2GM^2}{\pi R^4}$

where:

* $P_c$ is the central pressure.
* $G$ is the gravitational constant.
* $M$ is the stellar mass.
* $R$ is the stellar radius.

The relationship shows that the mass of a star is strongly connected to the pressure required in its interior.

For the Sun, numerical solutions from the Standard Solar Model give:

$P_c \approx 2.4 \times 10^{17}\ \mathrm{dyn\ cm^{-2}}$

This gives us an idea of the enormous pressure present in the solar interior.

---

# 19. Central Temperature

Higher pressure is associated with higher central temperature.

The research document gives the scaling:

$T_c \propto \frac{M}{R}$

where $T_c$ is the central temperature.

For the Sun, the Standard Solar Model gives approximately:

$T_c \approx 1.6 \times 10^7\ \mathrm{K}$

So the chain of reasoning is:

**higher mass → stronger gravitational requirements → higher central pressure → higher central temperature.**

This is one of the key connections between a star's mass and its internal physics.

---

# 20. Mass and the Length of a Stellar Life

The consequences do not stop at central pressure and temperature.

The research document states that mass and lifespan are inversely related.

The most massive stars have the **shortest biographies**.

The idea is that a star's initial mass sets the pace at which its evolutionary story unfolds.

Thus, two stars can begin their lives with very different masses and consequently experience very different evolutionary timescales.

Mass is therefore not merely a measure of how much material a star contains.

It is a major determinant of the star's evolutionary history.

---

# 21. The Mirror Principle: Core Contraction and Envelope Expansion

As a star evolves, its internal structure changes.

One of the most important ideas used to understand these changes is the **Mirror Principle**.

The research document presents the Mirror Principle as a consequence of the **Virial Theorem** and conservation of energy.

The total energy is written as:

$W = E_i + E_g$

where:

* $W$ is the total energy.
* $E_i$ is internal energy.
* $E_g$ is gravitational energy.

The key is that the core and envelope can respond in opposite ways as the star evolves.

---

# 22. Core Exhaustion

Consider what happens when the nuclear fuel in the core becomes depleted.

The core begins to contract.

As the core contracts, its gravitational energy decreases.

That change in gravitational energy releases energy.

So core contraction does not simply mean “the core gets smaller.”

It is an energetic process.

The change in gravitational energy becomes part of the energy budget of the evolving star.

---

# 23. Energy Partitioning

The research document gives the luminosity relation:

$L = -(\gamma - 1)\frac{dE_g}{dt}$

For a monatomic gas:

$\gamma = \frac{5}{3}$

The released gravitational energy is partitioned.

According to the research document, **half of the liberated energy heats the core**, while the other half is either radiated away or used to drive expansion of the outer layers.

This is an important consequence of the energy relations governing the stellar interior.

The core's contraction therefore has consequences far beyond the core itself.

---

# 24. The Mirror Effect

Here is where the name **Mirror Principle** becomes intuitive.

As the core contracts:

* the core becomes **denser**,
* the core becomes **hotter**.

At the same time, the envelope responds in the opposite direction:

* the envelope expands,
* it becomes more tenuous,
* it becomes cooler.

So we have:

**Core:** contracts → denser → hotter

**Envelope:** expands → more tenuous → cooler

This opposing behaviour is the “mirror” effect.

The research document connects this evolutionary change with the star's movement toward the **Red Giant branch** of the H-R diagram.

This is one of the most important examples of how the H-R diagram represents an actual physical transformation inside the star.

---

# 25. From Internal Change to Movement on the H-R Diagram

This gives us a deeper way to think about the H-R diagram.

A star does not move across the diagram because it is physically travelling through space.

Its position changes because its **physical properties change**.

If its internal structure changes, its temperature and luminosity can change.

Those changes alter its position on the H-R diagram.

For example, when the core contracts and the envelope expands, the star moves toward the Red Giant branch.

Therefore:

**internal structural evolution → changing stellar properties → changing H-R diagram position.**

The H-R diagram is consequently a map of stellar evolution as well as a classification tool.

---

# 26. The Hayashi Line: The Forbidden Zone

Another important region of the H-R diagram is defined by the **Hayashi Line**.

The research document describes the Hayashi Line as the **“forbidden zone”** of the H-R diagram.

It represents the boundary for fully convective stellar models.

For a given mass, no hydrostatic-equilibrium solution exists to the right of this line, corresponding to lower surface temperatures.

This makes the Hayashi Line fundamentally different from simply being another classification boundary.

It represents a physical limit on the possible structure of a star.

---

# 27. Why Does the Hayashi Line Exist?

To understand the Hayashi Line, we need to think about how energy travels through a star.

A star must transport energy from its interior toward its surface.

One possible mechanism is **radiative transport**.

But as a star approaches the Hayashi limit, its opacity increases.

Higher opacity makes radiative energy transport inefficient.

At that point, **convection** becomes the dominant mechanism for transporting energy.

So the Hayashi Line is closely connected to the transition between different energy-transport regimes.

---

# 28. Fully Convective Young Stars

The Hayashi Line is particularly important for understanding young stars.

A star forming from a protostellar cloud initially descends along the Hayashi Line while it is **fully convective**.

During this stage, convection is the dominant energy-transport mechanism.

The star is therefore not yet simply sitting on the main sequence.

It is still evolving toward the conditions required for its later stable state.

---

# 29. From the Hayashi Line Toward the ZAMS

As the young star continues to evolve, its interior becomes hotter.

Eventually, radiative energy transport becomes possible.

The star then moves toward the left on the H-R diagram, toward the ZAMS.

This represents an important physical transition:

**fully convective energy transport → radiative transport → movement toward the ZAMS.**

The H-R diagram therefore allows us to connect a star's position with the changing physical mechanism responsible for transporting energy through its interior.

---

# 30. The Stellar Biography So Far

We can now put the major ideas together.

A star begins its biography in a changing physical state.

As it evolves, its position on the H-R diagram reflects its temperature and luminosity.

Its spectral class tells us about its surface temperature and the resulting state of matter in its atmosphere.

Its mass influences the pressure and temperature inside it.

Hydrostatic equilibrium describes the balance between gravitational forces and internal pressure.

The ZAMS represents the beginning of the main-sequence stage after the initial contraction phase.

Later, when nuclear fuel is exhausted in the core, the core can contract while the envelope expands — the Mirror Principle.

The star can then move toward the Red Giant region.

For young stars, the Hayashi Line provides a boundary associated with fully convective models and the transition toward radiative energy transport.

The H-R diagram ties all of these pieces together into one evolutionary map.

# 32. Important Stellar Timescales

The research document gives two characteristic timescales.

### Hydrostatic Timescale

For the Sun:

$\tau_{hydr} \approx 27\ \mathrm{minutes}$

This is the characteristic hydrostatic timescale given in the research document.

### Kelvin–Helmholtz Timescale

For the Sun:

$\tau_{KH} \approx 1.6 \times 10^7\ \mathrm{years}$

The research document uses this timescale when describing the transition to the ZAMS, where the nuclear timescale takes over from the Kelvin–Helmholtz timescale.

The enormous difference between these timescales emphasizes that different physical processes in a star operate over very different characteristic durations.

# 33. Mean Molecular Weight

The research document also identifies the **mean molecular weight**, represented by $\mu$, as an important stellar quantity.

It also uses $\mu_e$ for the mean molecular weight per free electron.

These quantities change as hydrogen is processed into helium.

Therefore, as a star evolves and its composition changes, its mean molecular weight changes as well.

This provides another connection between the internal evolution of a star and the quantities used to describe its structure.

# 35. The Complete Conceptual Chain

The most useful way to remember this module is as a chain of connected ideas.

A star has a certain **mass**.

Its mass determines the gravitational conditions within it.

Those gravitational conditions determine the pressure required to maintain equilibrium.

The pressure is connected to the central temperature.

The internal temperature and structure determine how energy is produced and transported.

Energy eventually reaches the surface and is radiated away.

The surface radiation gives us information about the star's effective temperature and luminosity.

Those quantities place the star on the H-R diagram.

As the star evolves, its internal structure changes.

Its core and envelope can respond differently.

The resulting changes in temperature and luminosity move the star to a different location on the H-R diagram.

This is why the H-R diagram can function as a **biography of a star**.

# 36. What the H-R Diagram Is Really Telling Us

At first glance, the H-R diagram looks like a graph.

But the deeper interpretation is much more interesting.

A point on the diagram represents a particular combination of stellar properties.

That combination is connected to the star's internal structure.

Therefore, locating a star on the diagram allows us to ask:

* What is its surface temperature?
* What is its luminosity?
* What spectral class does it belong to?
* What physical conditions might exist inside it?
* What stage of its evolutionary biography does its position represent?

The H-R diagram is therefore a way of translating **observable properties into information about stellar structure and evolution**.

That is why the research document calls it the **“Cosmic Map.”**

# 40. Final Picture: Reading a Star's Biography

The H-R diagram gives us a way to read a star's story.

Its **effective temperature** tells us about the radiation coming from its surface.

Its **luminosity** tells us about its energy output.

Its **spectral class** gives us information about the physical state of matter in its atmosphere.

Its **mass** influences its central pressure and temperature and helps determine the pace of its evolutionary life.

Its internal structure is maintained through **hydrostatic equilibrium**, where pressure balances gravity.

As its internal fuel and structure change, the star can undergo **core contraction and envelope expansion**, producing the Mirror effect.

Young stars can begin their journey along the **Hayashi Line**, where convection dominates, before moving toward the **Zero-Age Main Sequence** as their interiors become hot enough for radiative transport.

So the H-R diagram is not merely a chart containing coloured dots.

It is a way of connecting what we can observe from outside a star with the physical processes taking place inside it.

That is what makes it a **cosmic map of a stellar biography**.
